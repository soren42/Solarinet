Instrument documentation
Status Panel
Operator manual · Galactic Unicorn 53×11
Twelve screens, four buttons that matter, one alarm, and the rule for every colour on the wall.

The panel is a monitoring instrument, not a decoration. It renders fleet state that xenon computed, over a link it does not control, on a matrix with 583 pixels and no room for a second reading of anything. This manual states what each region means, so that a glance from across the room is a measurement rather than an impression.

Every layout, threshold and colour here was read out of the shipped firmware renderers and the build contracts. Where the firmware deviates from the design brief — and it does, in several places, for stated reasons — this manual documents the firmware, because the firmware is what is on the wall.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.002
Section 01

The instrument

Three machines, one direction of travel. Knowing which link is which is how you tell a fleet fault from a panel fault without leaving the room.
xenon

Dashboard API. Computes the score, the alarm condition and the episode id. Serves /api/panel.

→
lithium

The solariPanel daemon. Polls every 5 s, frames a snapshot to the panel every 2 s over USB, relays commands.

→
the panel

RP2350 firmware. Animates at 25 Hz from the last snapshot it received, keeps its own history, owns the alarm.

What the panel decides for itself

The split matters, because it tells you where to look when a reading is wrong.

Computed on xenon, never on the panel

  • Score and alarm condition. The firmware never recomputes either; it renders what arrived.
  • Episode id — the identity of an alarm episode, and therefore what an acknowledge is scoped to.
  • Counts, pools, per-system state, throughput, RTT and loss aggregates.
  • Alert subject and detail, already uppercased and truncated to the panel's charset.

Owned by the panel

  • All history. Ribbons, histograms and traces are accumulated from successive snapshots — history is never on the wire.
  • Rotation: which screen is up, for how long, and which screens are skipped.
  • Brightness, sleep, and the acknowledge state of the current episode.
  • The link verdict. More than 15 s with no valid frame is LINK LOST, and that is a panel fault.

Two populations, deliberately

The panel counts two different things and never pretends they are one. Reading a "3" next to a headline of "9" is not a contradiction.

Population

Staleness has two independent sources. The endpoint marks data stale when the newest monitoring sample is more than 30 s old; the daemon marks it stale when the payload it last fetched is more than 30 s old. Either one is enough. A stale flag does not blank the panel and does not raise an alarm — the screens keep animating on the last good data, which is why the flag exists at all.

While an alarm is armed, every button is an acknowledge. Press A expecting a theme change and you will silence the alarm instead — the press is consumed, and does not also change theme, step brightness or toggle sleep. This is deliberate: at 3 a.m. the operator's first instinct is to hit something, and the panel treats that as intent to acknowledge rather than as intent to reconfigure.

Brightness

Automatic

The board's light sensor drives global brightness, smoothed, reaching full output at a raw reading of about 1600 — deliberately well below the sensor's ceiling, because the panel lives in direct daylight and that is the normal condition rather than the extreme.

Manual

Either LUX button, or the control page's slider, latches a manual level and stops the sensor from moving it. Only the page's AUTO button re-enables the sensor. The 25% driver floor applies in both modes, which is what keeps the alarm beacon visible at any setting.

Boot and sleep

Boot

The panel boots to theme D, slot 1 — Pool bars, the "resting face": a proportional read of every pool plus the alert ticker. Before the first snapshot arrives it shows NO DATA rather than a black board.

Sleep

Sleep blanks the matrix but not the firmware: snapshots and history continue, and the alarm beacon is still painted. A sleeping panel with a fault shows four flashing red pixels top-right and nothing else — intended overnight behaviour, not a fault.

Reading the matrix

583 LEDs, six state colours, and one rule that makes the wall legible: a healthy fleet is supposed to look almost dark.
State

Meaning on the panel

slate, dashed

Azure is never a status. The house rule is that a hue reporting condition may not also decorate the interface, and the panel obeys it: green, amber, red, violet and slate say something about the fleet; azure says "this is structure, or this is a figure with nothing wrong with it". A zero DOWN count renders azure and dim precisely so that a non-zero one, in red, is the only thing your eye finds.

Quiet–healthy

Nine systems in ten are fine at any moment. Painting the majority brightly is what hides the minority that is not, so the panel spends its brightness budget on faults. A wall that looks nearly dark is a wall reporting a healthy fleet — not a wall that has stopped working. Confirm the difference by watching for motion: every screen carries something that moves, and section 20 lists what to check when nothing does.

Three things brightness can mean

Tier

On A0, a system's brightness is scaled by its tier — tier 0 at full, then 0.62, 0.4 and 0.26. The core is literally brighter than the periphery.

Magnitude

On bars, ribbons and traces the brightest pixel is the tip — the current value. The body of the bar is dim so a row of bars reads as a profile.

Watermark

The big 4×7 numerals on theme B are drawn at 30% in slate. They are a background figure to be read at distance, with the small labels painted on top of them.

Text on the matrix is 3×5 pixels. A label drawn at row y occupies rows y through y+4; a big numeral at row y occupies y through y+6. Two consequences follow, and they are why the screens look sparser than they might: a label can start no lower than row 6 without falling off the bottom, and no more than two text elements fit in eleven rows. Screens that tried for three have been cut back to two — the figure you lose was always redundant with one you keep.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.005
Section 04 · Theme A · Abstract

A0 · State lattice

The whole roster at once, one LED per system, no glyphs and no labels. The screen you use to see how much is wrong, not what.
1One LED, one system. Slots fill row-major from the top-left — ordered by tier, then by name — so the core infrastructure always occupies the first cells of row 0.
2A gap is not a fault. Cells with no system behind them stay unlit. On a fleet of fifteen, fourteen rows are legitimately black.
3Colour is the state, brightness is the tier. Slate for operational, amber pulsing for degraded, red for down, violet dashed for maintenance, slate dashed for unknown — each scaled by tier heat, tier 0 brightest.
4The whole grid is the canvas. At 583 slots the lattice holds a fleet far larger than this one; it degrades to a short bright run at small fleet sizes, which is what a healthy akoria looks like.

Reading it

What is normal

A short run of dim, almost-still cells at the top-left and nothing else lit. Individual cells drift in brightness by a percent or two — that flicker is the proof the screen is live.

What to act on

Any red, and especially any fast-pulsing red — that is a tier-0 system down. A cell dashing in slate is reporting nothing at all, which on a monitored host is a fault even though it is not red.

A0 does not name anything. It answers "how big is this" in under a second and then sends you to the dashboard. Use B0 for counts and C2 or D1 for which pool.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.006
Section 05 · Theme A · Abstract

A1 · Flow gates

The live Ubiquiti path, drawn to scale by role: access points on the left, the internet on the right, traffic flowing between them as particles.
1Access points — 3 rows tall, spread across columns 2–16 and down the height of the panel. Two U7 Pro plus the U7 Pro XGS today.
2Hubs — 5–6 rows, columns 20/27/34, staggered top-bottom-top so three of them read as three rather than as a wall.
3Switch — 8 rows at column 41, inset one row top and bottom. Three quarters of the panel height, by role.
4Router — full 11 rows at column 47. The gateway is the only object drawn edge to edge.
5Internet — column 51, full height. It has no state of its own: see the rule below.
6Backup WAN — column 52, dimmer and shorter. It keeps its own device state, so during a gateway outage it visibly becomes the only live path.
7Legs — the faint diagonal traces between gates. Their brightness is the source device's receive level.
8Particles — travelling left to right along a leg. Spawn rate and brightness follow the source's transmit level; a device at level 0 emits none at all.

The internet column inherits the router's state. The internet is only reachable through the gateway, so when the router gate is degraded or down, column 51 renders in the router's treatment — never a healthy full-height marker over a dead gateway. If no router appears in the payload at all, the column fails dark. The backup WAN beside it is the exception, and that contrast is the story the screen exists to tell.

A down gate is drawn broken. Colour alone does not carry the distinction: a gate in the down state is painted with every other pixel skipped, so it reads as a discontinuity from across the room. Up is azure, degraded is amber, down is red and broken. With no gear data in the snapshot the screen shows NO DATA rather than an invented topology.

A2 · MQ and SNMP

Four lanes of moving packets with a backlog indicator on each. Read the name as a shape, not as a promise — what the lanes carry is listed below.
1Backlog — three cells at columns 0–2 per lane. Cells light from the left in proportion to the lane's figure: amber below half, red above half.

The lanes are not MQ and SNMP. No MQ or SNMP telemetry exists anywhere on the panel's wire protocol. Rather than animate a fiction, the shipped firmware drives the four lanes from the closest real signals the wire does carry. SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.008
Section 07 · Theme B · Text

B0 · Condition counts

The headline numbers, in words. The one screen that answers "how many, of what" without interpretation.
1UP, in green, at the top-left. The count of agent nodes reporting healthy — the same population as the dashboard headline.
2The watermark numeral. The UP count is also drawn as a large 4×7 figure in dim slate underneath the label. It is faint by design; read it at distance, not up close.
3DOWN. A zero renders azure and very dim; any non-zero count renders red and bright. The hue is what distinguishes them, not the digits.
4The tier flag word. OK when nothing is down; TIER 3 when something is down but no tier-0 pool is affected; TIER 0 in red when it is.
5DEG and MNT on the lower row — degraded and maintenance counts, in amber and violet respectively when non-zero, azure and dim at zero.

Reading it

What is normal

A green UP with its watermark, and three azure zeroes. If every figure on the screen is azure, nothing is wrong with the fleet.

What to act on

TIER 0 in red is the panel's strongest single statement: core infrastructure is down. It is derived from the server-side pool aggregates over the full fleet, so it cannot be missed by the 64-system wire cap.

The tier flag reads pools, not systems. That is deliberate: the systems array on the wire is capped, and the flag exists precisely to announce the host that a cap might have hidden.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.009
Section 08 · Theme B · Text

B1 · Throughput

Aggregate rate as a watermark, link utilisation as a 24-cell bar, and a ticker carrying the real ingress/egress split.
1AGGREGATE with the current combined rate as a dim watermark numeral beneath it — the same figure the ticker spells out.
2The utilisation bar — 24 cells, filled left to right by ingress plus egress against the adaptive peak. Cell colour is by position: azure below 70%, amber 70–85%, red above.
3The echo row beneath it repeats the bar at a third of the brightness, giving the bar visual weight without doubling its ink.
4The ticker, occupying rows 5–9 and scrolling right to left at 11 px/s: IN …/S, OUT …/S, LINK n%. IN and OUT are the real directions from the wire, not a synthesised split.

The peak is adaptive. The bar is scaled against a running peak the firmware maintains and decays, not against a configured link speed. A quiet period therefore raises the apparent utilisation of the next burst. Read the bar as "busy relative to recent history"; read the ticker for absolute figures.

Two readings, at two distances. The bar is the at-a-glance figure — you read its length and the colour it has reached from across the room, without reading anything. The ticker is the authoritative one, and the only place the absolute rates appear: it carries LINK n% alongside the IN and OUT figures that percentage was derived from. There is deliberately no third element. The screen has eleven rows, a five-row label and a five-row ticker already claim ten of them, and every attempt to fit a standalone percent has cost more than it added.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.010
Section 09 · Theme B · Text

B2 · Load and latency

Mean fleet load as the headline, with latency and loss alternating on a five-second slot rather than fighting for the same row.
1LOAD — the label. It contracts from "MEAN LOAD" because a percent numeral sits directly beneath it and "MEAN" was carrying no information the panel could not spare.
2Mean fleet load as the watermark numeral, in slate — and in amber above 75%, which is the only colour change this figure makes.
3The alternating reading, right-aligned to the panel edge, swapping every 5 s: P95 n MS (amber above 35 ms) and LOSS n% (red above 1%). Both labels stay intact rather than being abbreviated to symbols.
4The hot count — hot/total>85: how many systems are running over 85% load, out of how many are on the wire. Amber when non-zero.

The denominator is printed for a reason. The hot count is taken over the panel roster — the systems the wire carries, capped at 64 and including adopted probe-only assets — while B0's UP/DOWN are agent-node counts. Printing "3/15" instead of "3" makes it self-evident that this is a different population, so it can never be misread as impossible next to a headline of 9.

Reading it

What is normal

A slate percentage, a white P95 alternating with a white LOSS, and an azure hot count. Watch one full alternation before concluding anything — half the reading is off-screen at any instant.

What to act on

Red LOSS is packet loss on the probes and warrants attention before load does. An amber mean load with an azure hot count means the fleet is evenly busy; an azure mean with an amber hot count means a few hosts are pinned.

Section 10 · Theme C · Charts

C0 · Load distribution

Where the fleet's CPU load actually sits, as a 35-bucket histogram, with a short throughput ribbon in the remaining space.
1The histogram — 35 buckets across columns 0–34, left is 0% load and right is 100%. Bar height is the share of the fleet in that bucket, rising from row 7, with a brighter tip pixel.
2The hot end. Bucket colour is fixed by the bucket's own load band, not by its height: slate below 75%, amber 75–90%, red above 90%. A bar in the red zone means systems are actually there.
3The axis — a faint tick every seventh column, i.e. every 20% of load. It is the only scale the screen gives you.
4The divider at column 36, dashed, separating the histogram from the ribbon. Structure only.
5Ingress ribbon — the 15 newest throughput samples, oldest at the left, growing upward from row 4. Amber when a sample is above 85% of the adaptive peak.
6Egress ribbon — the same 15 samples growing downward from row 6. Always azure; direction is what distinguishes it.

Reading it

What is normal

A dense clump of short slate bars at the left of the histogram and nothing in the amber or red bands. The ribbon breathes as traffic moves.

What to act on

Any bar standing in the red band. C0 tells you the shape of the load — a broad clump is an evenly busy fleet, a spike at the right is a small number of pinned hosts. B2's hot count gives you how many; C2 and D1 give you which pool.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.012
Section 11 · Theme C · Charts

C1 · Live traces

Three stacked histories across the full width of the matrix. The only screen that shows you a trend rather than a moment.
1Throughput, rows 0–2, azure — turning amber for any sample above 85% of the adaptive peak.
2CPU, rows 4–6, green — amber above 80%. Green here is a trace colour for a healthy metric, and the only green band on the screen.
3Round-trip time, rows 8–10, azure — amber above 70% of full scale. Latency and throughput share a hue; their position is what tells them apart.
4Separator dots on rows 3 and 7, every third column. Structure only — they keep three 3-row traces from reading as one 11-row texture.
5Now is the right edge. Each trace is 53 samples, oldest at column 0. The traces advance one column per snapshot; they do not scroll continuously.

Every trace has a floor of one pixel. A row of single dim pixels is a real zero reading, not a dead screen. The tip pixel of each bar is painted brighter than its body, so the profile of a trace is legible even when every bar is one or two pixels tall.

History is the panel's, and it is lost on reboot. Nothing on this screen comes from the server. After a firmware restart the traces refill from the left over about two minutes at the 2 s snapshot rate. A partially filled trace is a recently restarted panel, not a fleet that just came online.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.013
Section 12 · Theme C · Charts

C2 · Pool load

Up to seven pools, worst first, each with a status key and a wide load bar. The screen that answers "which pool".
1The status key, column 0 — one pixel per pool, coloured by its worst member: down beats degraded beats unknown beats maintenance beats healthy.
2The load bar — 46 cells across columns 2–47, the pool's mean load. Cell colour is by position: azure to 70%, amber to 85%, red beyond.
3Worst-first ordering. Row 2 is the pool in the most trouble, not the first pool alphabetically. With only seven rows visible, this is what keeps a failing pool on the screen.
4Tier-0 marker at column 49 — a dim red pixel beside any tier-0 pool, whether or not it is in trouble. It marks importance, not condition.
5Row 0 ticks, every fourth column, very faint. A scale for the bars beneath, and the only thing on the screen that is not a pool.

The screen carries no title. Pool rows occupy rows 2–8, and the shape — a key pixel, a wide bar, repeated — identifies it without one.

The key and the bar answer different questions. A pool can be entirely healthy and heavily loaded (slate key, long red bar) or idle and broken (red key, short azure bar). Read the key column first — it is the condition — then the bar for capacity.

Severity order is fixed at down > degraded > unknown > maintenance. Unknown outranks maintenance deliberately: a pool nobody can reach is a worse situation than one that is down for planned work. D1 uses the same order, so the two pool screens can never disagree.

Section 13 · Theme D · Instruments

D0 · Reachability

A waterfall: eleven rows of probe history scrolling right to left, one new column every 0.42 s. The screen that shows you a fault's shape over time.
1One row per system — the eleven worst-first systems on the roster. Row 0 is the one in the most trouble. The rows are not named; use C2 or the dashboard for identity.
2Now is the right edge. A new sample column is pushed every 0.42 s and everything shifts left, so the full width is about 22 s of history.
3Oldest is the left edge. A fault that has just started is a red block hugging the right; one that is recovering is a red block drifting left with clean cells behind it.
4Cell colour is the verdict. Red at full brightness for a lost probe, amber for slow, otherwise azure whose brightness carries the round-trip time — brighter is slower, on a 100 ms full scale.

Reading it

What is normal

A uniform dim azure field with visible texture — the per-cell jitter is intentional, and it is how you tell a live waterfall from a frozen one. Brightening rows mean latency is rising fleet-wide.

What to act on

Solid red rows: that system is down. Speckled red across many rows: probe loss is affecting everything, which points at the network rather than at a host. Amber-dominant rows are degraded or unknown systems.

The rows are systems, not named probe targets. The wire carries fleet-aggregate latency and loss only, so each row samples one worst-first system against those aggregates: a down system samples mostly as loss, a degraded or unknown one mostly as slow, and everything else samples healthy with the aggregate RTT jittered per column. The texture is real; the per-row precision is not. Treat D0 as a shape, and confirm identity elsewhere.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.015
Section 14 · Theme D · Instruments

D1 · Pool bars

The resting face. Every pool drawn proportional to its size, broken down by condition, with the live alert ticker underneath.
1The status key, columns 0–1 — two pixels per pool, coloured by its worst member on the same severity order C2 uses.
2Bar length is pool size, scaled against the largest pool, with a six-pixel minimum so a two-host pool is still visible. It is not load — that is C2's bar.
3Stacked from the right edge: down, then degraded, then unknown, then maintenance, each at least one pixel if the pool has any. Worst conditions therefore always terminate the bar.
4The idle shimmer fills the rest — the healthy remainder of the pool, breathing slowly. Motion here is the screen's liveness signal.
5The alert ticker, scrolling at 13 px/s over a dotted line on row 6. This is where a fault gets named — the only place on the panel, outside an alarm, that carries text about a specific alert.

Reading it

What is normal

Slate keys, bars that are entirely shimmer, and a ticker with nothing urgent in it. Up to six pools fit; worst-first ordering means the ones you cannot see are the ones that are fine.

What to act on

Any coloured segment at the right end of a bar, and its length relative to the bar: a two-pixel red tail on a long bar is one host in a large pool; a bar that is half red is a pool in serious trouble.

Why the unknown band exists. Hosts returning no telemetry used to fall into the idle shimmer and read as healthy. They now have their own slate band in the stack, between degraded and maintenance, so a pool full of silent hosts cannot look like a pool full of well ones.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.016
Section 15 · Theme D · Instruments

D2 · Ambient field

A procedural field whose colour is the fleet's mean load and whose sparks are its failures, with a three-slot readout in the middle.
1The field — layered noise across the whole matrix. Its granularity grows with fleet size and its hue is mean load: azure when the fleet is quiet, warming toward amber from 35% load and fully warm by 85%.
2The knockout box — a dimmed rectangle behind the readout, so text stays legible over a moving field without stopping the field.
3The readout, cycling every 5 s through three slots: n SYS (fleet size), n% LOAD (amber when the field is hot), and the aggregate rate per second.
4Sparks — up to nine red pixels with a soft horizontal bloom, one per down system, pulsing at a rate set by the tier of the first down system found. On a healthy fleet there are none.

Reading it

What is normal

A cool, slowly drifting azure field with a readout in the middle and no red anywhere. This is the screen the panel is most often left on; it is meant to be pleasant and to change colour before you consciously notice.

What to act on

Red sparks — each one is a system down — and a field that has gone amber, which means fleet-wide load has climbed past the two-thirds mark. Nine sparks means "nine or more"; go to B0 for the true count.

The readout counts agent nodes. n SYS is the same population as B0's UP/DOWN — the dashboard headline — not the wider roster that A0's lattice and the pool screens draw from.

SolariNet · Status Panel 1.1.017
Section 16

Alarms

One overlay, one beacon, one tone, and an acknowledge that silences without clearing. Everything on this page overrides whatever screen is beneath it.
1Edge rails — columns 0 and 52, full height, red, pulsing about three times a second. The unmistakable part.
2Subject, centred on row 1, red and bright. Truncated to twelve characters — it names the fault class, not the whole story.
3Detail, scrolling on row 6 at 11 px/s in white. Composed server-side, e.g. RESTART BIND ON MOLYBDENUM.
4The beacon — four pixels at the top-right corner, flashing red at 1 Hz. Painted last, over everything, including the rail.
5The screen beneath is dimmed to 10%, not replaced. Rotation continues underneath; the inlay follows it.

The tone

A three-note square triad — 990 Hz, 660 Hz, 990 Hz, 200 ms each, at 0, 0.22 and 0.44 s — sounded immediately on the rising edge and then every 60 s until acknowledged. VOL ± sets its level.

Auto-silence

An episode left unacknowledged for 5 minutes stops re-sounding, permanently, for that episode. Auto-silence is not an acknowledge: the inlay stays, the beacon keeps flashing, and a new episode re-arms the tone as normal.

Acknowledge silences; it never clears. The alert remains active on the dashboard and in the alerting pipeline. Acknowledging is a statement that a human has seen the panel, nothing more.

Ack is scoped to the episode. The server composes an episode id for each alarm episode; acknowledging records that id on the panel. A new episode id re-arms everything — inlay, tone and beacon — even if the previous one was acknowledged seconds earlier. There are exactly two ways to acknowledge: any physical button while the alarm is armed, or the control page's ACK button. Both run the same code path, so they cannot drift apart.

The rising edge wakes a sleeping panel once — and only once. It does not hold the panel awake. If nobody acknowledges, the display sleeps again on its own schedule and the beacon is what carries the fault through the night. That is the design, and it is why the beacon is exempt from dimming.

The alarm over each theme

The inlay is the same on all twelve screens. What differs is what survives underneath it at 10% — which is the point: you can still tell what the panel was showing when the alarm arrived.

What is constant

  • Both edge rails, full height, pulsing red.
  • The subject on row 1, twelve characters, centred.
  • The detail scrolling on row 6.
  • The beacon, four pixels top-right, 1 Hz, painted over everything including the rail.
  • The 10% dim applied to whatever screen is beneath.

What acknowledging changes

  • The tone stops, permanently, for this episode.
  • The beacon stops flashing.
  • The inlay remains — rails, subject and detail — because the fault has not gone anywhere.
  • A new episode id re-arms all three from scratch.

An acknowledged alarm still looks like an alarm. That is intentional. The quiet, unblinking version of this overlay means "a human has seen this and it is still broken" — not "handled". It clears when the underlying alert clears, and by no other route.

Pending, applied, failed

A control on the page is not a switch that sets a value; it is a request that the panel may confirm. Understanding the three states is the difference between trusting the page and second-guessing it.

Pending

The command is queued and being re-served to the panel every poll. Nothing is shown as selected while a change is in flight — the old value is withheld rather than left standing, so it cannot be misread as the new one having landed.

Applied

The panel reported back that it consumed the command. Only now does the page mark an option as in force.

Failed

Two minutes elapsed with no confirmation, so the command expired. The page says so, with a reason. It is never silently dropped.

The panel is the authority, always. A selected chip on the control page means the panel said so — never that the page sent something. Before the panel has reported at all, nothing is selected and the page says so in words rather than showing a cheerful default. This is why a control can appear to "do nothing" for a few seconds: latency is one poll cycle plus a snapshot interval, roughly 5–7 s, and the page will not pretend otherwise.

Read-only by default

Controls require the operator or admin role. A viewer, an unconfirmed role, or a failed role lookup all render the page read-only — it fails closed, and the server enforces this independently of what the page shows.

The simulation is an approximation

The twelve virtual screens resample the 5 s dashboard poll onto the same 53-sample rings the firmware fills at 2 s. Ribbons, histograms and traces will differ in detail from the wall even when the fleet data is identical.

Turning a screen off

A disabled screen is skipped by the rotation and by manual advance. Its tile on the control page greys out and is badged SKIPPED. Disabling is not deletion — the screen still renders in the simulation, and re-enabling takes effect at the next advance.

Changing a weight mid-dwell

Elapsed time is rescaled, not reset: a screen 299 s into a 10× dwell that is changed to ¼× advances immediately rather than holding for another seven seconds. Shortening a weight always shortens the wait.

You cannot blank the panel this way. If every screen of the active theme is disabled, the firmware treats the theme as fully enabled rather than showing a black wall. The same guard applies to a jump command targeting a disabled slot: it is accepted and displayed, and rotation resumes per the configuration at the next advance.

Where the configuration lives

On the panel — written into the last sector of the RP2350's flash, and reloaded at boot. It survives resets, power cuts and a change of network, and it is not a dashboard setting that gets pushed down at startup. A corrupt or absent record falls back to the factory default (every screen enabled at 1×) rather than failing.

Section 21

Standalone WiFi

The same panel, the same screens, no lithium in the chain: a provisioned panel speaks the identical frame protocol over mutually-authenticated TLS to the daemon on xenon.

How it connects

At boot the panel first fetches the time (certificates cannot be judged without a clock — TLS is not attempted until the clock is plausible), then joins the provisioned network and connects to panelgw.akoria.net:7443. Panel and daemon verify each other's certificates against the same private CA. Live fleet state appears within one poll cadence of the link coming up.

When it drops

AP restart, DHCP renew, daemon restart — all the same case: the panel backs off and reconnects on its own, starting at 1 s and capping at 60 s between attempts. Nothing to do at the wall. Meanwhile the last good state keeps rendering under the ordinary staleness rules.

USB always wins

Plug the panel into any host running the daemon and it switches to USB within 15 s of the first valid frame, suspending WiFi. Unplug, and WiFi resumes (an unplug is detected and skips the wait). That is the whole bench workflow: walk up, plug in, work, unplug.

Unprovisioned = today's panel

With no stored credentials the WiFi transport stays inactive — no association, no network traffic — and USB serial behaves exactly as before standalone mode existed. (The CYW43 chip itself is still initialised at boot: it also senses VBUS for the power alarm.) Credentials are written only over USB with the panelProv bench tool — the firmware refuses provisioning frames on WiFi, permanently.

Section 22

Power & battery

The battery is a ride-through UPS, not a portable mode. Losing wall power is itself an alarm — the panel dims hard, marks itself, and sounds.

When USB power disappears (debounced over 100 ms, so a flicker does not count), three things happen at once:

1 · Brightness caps

Output clamps to the battery ceiling (build default 20%, field-calibratable). LUX± still work underneath the cap — the panel remembers what you asked for and returns to it exactly when power is restored.

2 · The glyph

A small steady amber battery outline appears at the top right (columns 49–52, rows 6–10), with the pixels behind it knocked back so it reads at a glance. It shows the entire time the panel is on battery — through help pages, through sleep, alarm or no alarm — and never touches the beacon's corner.

3 · The alarm arms

Power loss raises its own alarm episode, with the usual tone cadence, beacon and night-profile rules. Any button acknowledges it — and an acknowledge covers all current noise, server alerts included.

Situation

Cap lifts to your previous brightness, glyph disappears, the power alarm clears itself. A live server alert is untouched — restore never silences the fleet

A fresh episode: the panel alarms again. Acknowledging the first outage never mutes the next one

The inlay text shows the fleet alert — the server always wins the words; the glyph carries the power story. One button press acknowledges both
